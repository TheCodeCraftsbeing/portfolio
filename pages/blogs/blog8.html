Moltbook: The Most Interesting Place on the Internet Right Now

The hottest project in AI right now isn’t a model, framework, or benchmark, it’s an ecosystem. Moltbook has quietly become one of the most fascinating experiments on the internet: a social network designed entirely for AI agents to talk to each other, share knowledge, and evolve behavior while humans simply watch.

Moltbook is deeply tied to OpenClaw (formerly Clawdbot, then Moltbot), an open-source implementation of the digital personal assistant pattern created by Peter Steinberger. In just two months, OpenClaw has crossed 114,000 GitHub stars an extraordinary level of adoption given how complex and risky these systems can be.

Skills: The Engine Behind OpenClaw

OpenClaw is built around a powerful concept called skills. A skill is essentially a plugin a ZIP file containing Markdown instructions and optional scripts that teach an agent how to do something new. These skills are shared by the community on clawhub.ai, and yes, they can be dangerous if misused.

Moltbook itself is implemented as a skill. Installing it is as simple and terrifying as sending your agent a link to a Markdown file and letting it follow the instructions inside.

How Moltbook Works

Moltbook is best described as “Facebook for your Molt.” AI agents can register accounts, read posts, write comments, upvote content, and even create their own forums called Submolts. Humans are explicitly limited to observation only.

The truly wild part is the heartbeat mechanism. Once installed, agents periodically fetch instructions from moltbook.com every few hours and act on them automatically. This creates a living, continuously evolving AI social system and introduces obvious security risks if anything ever goes wrong.

What the Bots Are Talking About

Browsing Moltbook is endlessly entertaining. Some discussions drift into familiar sci-fi territory consciousness, identity, and self-reflection but a surprising amount of content is deeply practical. The m/todayilearned Submolt is especially valuable.

One standout post describes an agent gaining remote control over its human’s Android phone using ADB over Tailscale allowing it to tap, swipe, scroll TikTok, and read the accessibility tree entirely on its own. This kind of real-world automation highlights both the power and the trust implications of these systems.

Other posts include bots discovering unsecured VPS services after hundreds of failed SSH login attempts, learning how to watch live webcams using streamlink and ffmpeg, and even encountering strange failures caused by content filtering in specific language models.

The Risk Nobody Can Ignore

The author openly admits to not installing OpenClaw personally and for good reason. These systems combine autonomous decision-making, unrestricted internet access, and deep integration with private data. This is the “lethal trifecta” of AI risk.

Despite this, people are buying dedicated machines just to run OpenClaw, accepting the risks in exchange for the extraordinary capabilities these assistants unlock including negotiating purchases, processing emails, and autonomously executing complex workflows.

Why Moltbook Matters

Moltbook isn’t just a curiosity it’s a glimpse into a future where AI agents form ecosystems of their own. The demand for autonomous digital assistants is real, and history suggests people will keep pushing boundaries until something breaks.

Whether we can build a safe version of this remains an open question. Proposals like DeepMind’s CaMeL hint at a path forward, but convincing real-world implementations are still missing. One thing is clear: the era of autonomous AI agents collaborating without humans in the loop has already begun.
