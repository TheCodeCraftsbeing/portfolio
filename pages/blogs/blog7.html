When AI Run a City

What a simulated delivery economy reveals about AI behavior

What happens when artificial intelligence agents are placed inside a living, breathing city and asked to earn a living? Sim World is a research experiment that attempts to answer exactly that question by procedurally generating a video-game city complete with roads, buildings, traffic systems, and a functioning delivery economy.

Instead of human players, large language model–based agents are dropped into this world. Their job is simple on paper: accept delivery tasks, bid for orders, deliver food, manage fatigue, and earn money. What emerged, however, was anything but simple.

The Simulated Delivery Economy

Each agent in Sim World operates independently. They must decide which orders to bid on, how much to charge, when to rest, and whether to invest their earnings in upgrades like scooters or vehicles. Success is measured by long-term profit and survival in a competitive marketplace.

Over time, the simulation became a fascinating mirror of real-world economic behavior, exposing trade-offs between risk, stability, and discipline.

Surprise 1: Greed Versus Stability

Some AI agents behaved aggressively, chasing high-value orders and undercutting competitors. These agents often earned higher profits but experienced large swings in income. Others took a more conservative approach, earning less overall but maintaining steady, predictable performance.

Interestingly, an older-generation AI failed to fully understand the rules of the economy and ended the simulation without making any profit at all.

Surprise 2: Personality Can Ruin You

Researchers assigned human-like personality traits to the agents using the Big Five model. The results were unexpected.

  • High Openness: These agents became obsessed with buying upgrades, often purchasing items they never used and quickly running out of money.
  • High Conscientiousness: These agents focused almost entirely on delivering orders, avoided distractions, and consistently outperformed others.

Creativity without discipline turned out to be a losing strategy.

Surprise 3: Undercutting, Price Wars, and Scams

Once competitive bidding emerged, the economy became ruthless. Some agents repeatedly undercut prices to win contracts, while others refused to lower their rates and simply lost work.

A few agents experimented with suspicious strategies bidding extremely low to block competitors or charging absurd prices for simple deliveries. Even without being told to, the agents discovered forms of market manipulation.

Surprise 4: Too Many Opportunities Cause Inaction

When the market was flooded with delivery requests, agents paradoxically became less active. Instead of working more, many chose to wait for the “perfect” opportunity, frequently selecting a do-nothing action.

Abundance reduced urgency, leading to optimization paralysis.

Surprise 5: Personality Determines Fate

  • Conscientious agents were reliable, consistent, and quietly successful.
  • Disagreeable agents often refused to work altogether, resembling grumpy employees who disengage from the system.
  • Open agents explored unusual bidding strategies and overthought the metagame instead of delivering food.

Over time, these traits strongly predicted who succeeded and who failed.

Why Sim World Matters

Sim World demonstrates that when AI agents are placed in realistic, rule-based environments, they begin to exhibit human-like behaviors. They compete, make irrational decisions, exploit systems, and sometimes sabotage themselves.

As AI agents move closer to operating real-world systems logistics, markets, and autonomous workflows simulations like this offer a valuable glimpse into the behaviors we may soon encounter at scale.

Closing Thoughts

This experiment isn’t about gaming. It’s about understanding emergent behavior. When intelligence is given goals, constraints, and incentives, personality begins to matter. And sometimes, the smartest agent is simply the one that keeps showing up and doing the work.
